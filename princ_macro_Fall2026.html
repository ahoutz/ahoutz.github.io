Foundations and Opportunity Costs

Concepts: In this section, we cover the foundations of economics. What is economics? What are the pillars of modern economic thought? How do economists study the economy? Why is the concept of opportunity cost so important? This section culminates in the production possibilities frontier and the idea of specialization.

Supply and Demand

Concepts: We introduce markets and market structures, focusing on competitive markets. We then build the supply and demand curves, provide an argument for the existence of market equilibrium, and study the impact of curve shifts on equilibrium. We end this section by conducting welfare analysis on free markets, markets with taxes and subsidies, and markets with price floors and ceilings.

Summary
GDP and CPI

Concepts: We transition from microeconomics into macroeconomics. We introduce GDP through circular flow, establishing that production always equals income and always equals expenditure. We then separate prices from quantities in the aggregate data by introducing real GDP and the GDP deflator. We study inflation by constructing the consumer price index and examining reasons inflation is costly. Lastly, we introduce the equation of exchange and briefly consider the link between the money supply and inflation.

Summary
Long Run Growth and Unemployment

Concepts: After Midterm I, we start thinking about how the economy grows. After looking at historical trends in GDP per capita, we build a simple production model that depends on technology, capital, and labor. We discuss whether this model can account for all differences between countries, and briefly consider the role of natural resources and political institutions on economic growth.

After studying growth, we think about each aspect of the simple production model starting with labor. We cover BLS definitions of the labor force and unemployment. We then disaggregate the unemployment statistics to recognize substantial underlying heterogeneity. We close this unit by thinking about structural, frictional, and cyclical unemployment.

Summary
Investment and Savings

Concepts: We next transition into thinking about capital. We cover the capital accumulation equation and the importance of investment for growing the capital stock. From the national accounts, we find that national savings equals investment in a closed economy. We then generalize to an open economy. Lastly, we model the matching of investors and savers using supply and demand. We build demand and supply for loanable funds by thinking about how the price of a loan, the real interest rate, influences investors' and savers' decisions.

International Trade

Concepts: Since the financial account offsets the current account, we take a brief detour and think about international trade. We model international trade from the perspective of a small open economy in a competitive market. Using supply and demand diagrams, we argue that free trade is welfare improving upon autarky. We then analyze various trade policies - tariffs, production subsidies, and quotas - to determine which, if any, can help the domestic economy.

Summary
The Keynesian Cross

Concepts: After Midterm II, we build the Keynesian Cross model. We posit a consumption function based on Keynes' observations in The General Theory. We then distinguish planned investment from unplanned investment. After solving for equilibrium using the expenditure equation for GDP, we can graph the Keynesian Cross. We discuss unplanned investment and how the economy adapts to shocks, or changes in planned aggregate expenditure.

Summary
Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply

Concepts: We take the concepts from the Keynesian Cross model and apply them to aggregate demand. After establishing that aggregate demand is downward sloping in (Y, P) space, we argue that aggregate supply is upward sloping due to sticky wages. Taking our simple production model, we argue that in the long run, aggregate supply is vertical at long-run, or potential, output. We then study how the model's equilibrium adjusts in response to various shocks, both in the short-run and the long-run.
